When a camera moves through a scene, each visible 3D point traces a path across the image plane. The **motion field** is the projection of this 3D motion onto the image: it assigns a 2D velocity to every image point. This post derives the motion field equation and shows what it reveals about scene depth and camera motion.

## Setup

Assume a rigid, static scene and a camera with translational velocity $\mathbf{V} = [V_x, V_y, V_z]^T$ and rotational velocity $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = [\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^T$. A scene point $\mathbf{P} = [X, Y, Z]^T$, expressed in the camera frame, projects to the normalized image point $\mathbf{p} = [x, y, 1]^T$:

$$
x = \frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = \frac{Y}{Z}, \qquad \mathbf{p} = \frac{1}{Z}\mathbf{P}
$$

## Derivation

**Step 1: Motion of the point.** Relative to the camera, a static point moves opposite to the camera's motion:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{P}} = -\boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{V}
$$

Expanding the cross product gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{X} &= -V_x - \omega_y Z + \omega_z Y \\
\dot{Y} &= -V_y - \omega_z X + \omega_x Z \\
\dot{Z} &= -V_z - \omega_x Y + \omega_y X
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2: Differentiate the projection.** Applying the quotient rule to $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{P}/Z$ gives $\dot{\mathbf{p}} = \dot{\mathbf{P}}/Z - (\dot{Z}/Z)\,\mathbf{p}$, or in components:

$$
\dot{x} = \frac{\dot{X} - x\dot{Z}}{Z}, \qquad \dot{y} = \frac{\dot{Y} - y\dot{Z}}{Z}
$$

**Step 3: Substitute.** Inserting the expressions from Step 1 and replacing $X/Z$ with $x$ and $Y/Z$ with $y$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{x} &= \frac{xV_z - V_x}{Z} + xy\,\omega_x - (1 + x^2)\,\omega_y + y\,\omega_z \\[6pt]
\dot{y} &= \frac{yV_z - V_y}{Z} + (1 + y^2)\,\omega_x - xy\,\omega_y - x\,\omega_z
\end{aligned}
$$

In matrix form, this is the **motion field equation**:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{p}} =
\underbrace{\frac{1}{Z}
\begin{bmatrix}
xV_z - V_x \\
yV_z - V_y
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{translational flow}}
+
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
xy & -(1 + x^2) & y \\
(1 + y^2) & -xy & -x
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{\text{rotational flow}}
$$

## Translation and Rotation

The two components behave very differently:

- **Translational flow depends on depth.** It scales with $1/Z$, so nearby points move faster in the image. This effect is known as motion parallax.
- **Rotational flow is independent of depth.** $Z$ does not appear in this term, so a purely rotating camera provides no information about scene structure.
- **Scale is ambiguous.** Translation and depth appear only as the ratio $\mathbf{V}/Z$, so a single camera cannot recover absolute scale from image motion alone.

## Focus of Expansion

Factoring $V_z$ out of the translational flow gives

$$
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{trans}} = \frac{V_z}{Z}
\begin{bmatrix}
x - V_x/V_z \\
y - V_y/V_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This flow is zero at a single image point, and every flow vector points along the line through it. This point is the **focus of expansion** (FOE), and it marks the camera's direction of travel:

$$
\text{FOE} = \left(\frac{V_x}{V_z},\; \frac{V_y}{V_z}\right)
$$

When $V_z = 0$, the FOE moves to infinity and the flow vectors become parallel.

The same pattern appears in the rendered scene at the top of this page, where the camera drives down a street while the cars drive slowly towards it. The static street expands from a single FOE, but the oncoming cars do not follow it: their own motion adds to the camera's, so they move faster than the street around them.

## Time to Contact

The **time to contact** $\tau$ is the time remaining before the camera reaches the plane of a point, assuming constant velocity:

$$
\tau = \frac{Z}{V_z}
$$

Taking the magnitude of the translational flow above and rearranging gives

$$
\tau = \frac{Z}{V_z} = \frac{\|\mathbf{p} - \text{FOE}\|}{\|\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{trans}}\|}
$$

Notably, **time to contact can be measured from a single moving camera without knowing either depth or speed**, even though neither can be recovered individually. It requires only the flow vector and its distance from the FOE. Among points at the same distance from the FOE, longer flow vectors therefore indicate a more imminent collision.

Time to contact is widely used for obstacle avoidance on small robots, and it also appears in nature. Gannets enter the sea like torpedoes: they plunge at high speed and must fold their wings just before impact. Their timing is consistent with a time-to-contact estimate from the expanding image of the water, rather than separate estimates of height and speed [1, 2].

## Motion Field vs. Optical Flow

A camera cannot measure the motion field directly. It observes only changing brightness patterns, and the apparent motion of these patterns is called **optical flow**. Optical flow is an observable approximation of the motion field, but the two are not always equal.

The reverse also occurs. A moving light changes the shading across a stationary sphere and its surroundings, which produces optical flow although the motion field is zero.

Local measurements add a further ambiguity. Through a small window, only the component of motion perpendicular to an edge can be observed. This is the **aperture problem**, and it explains why a barber pole's stripes appear to move vertically although the pole rotates horizontally.
