You point your phone at a skyline, hit **Pano**, wave your arm like you're conducting an invisible orchestra, and *bam* — one glorious, seam-free, ultra-wide photo. It feels like magic.

## 1. The naive plan (and why it faceplants)

"Easy," says past-you. "Just slide the right photo over until it lines up, and average the overlap."

Here is that exact plan, executed with confidence:

<figure class="pano-figure">
  <img src={base + "images/pano/02-naive.jpg"} alt="Naive overlap: ghosted double image with visible brightness bands" />
  <figcaption>Tower Bridge, as experienced after spinning in a chair for five minutes.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two problems, both fatal:

1. **Geometry.** The camera *rotated* between shots, so the right photo isn't just shifted — it's rotated and slightly keystoned. No amount of sliding fixes that. We need a *warp*.
2. **Photometry.** The two photos have different brightness (auto-exposure did that), so even where they *do* line up, you can see the seam.

The real pipeline fixes both:

1. 📍 **Find corners** in each image (Harris)
2. 🗺️ **Spread them out** (ANMS)
3. 🏷️ **Describe** each corner with a tiny fingerprint
4. 💘 **Match** fingerprints across the two images
5. 🎰 **Vote out the liars** (RANSAC) and fit a **homography**
6. 🌀 **Warp** one image onto the other
7. 🪄 **Blend** away the seam

Every step below follows the same rhythm: **intuition → code → result**, plus something you can poke at. All the code runs with just `numpy` and `opencv-python`; grab the <a href={base + "code/stitch.py"}>complete script</a> to run along.

```python

```

## 2. Find the corners 📍

To align two images, we need landmarks that we can find in *both* — distinctive points we could recognize again. What makes a point recognizable?

Imagine sliding a tiny window around the image and asking: *"if I nudge this window a little, does what's inside change?"*

- On flat **sky**: nudge it anywhere, nothing changes. Useless. A patch of sky could match a million other patches of sky.
- On an **edge**: nudge it *along* the edge, nothing changes. Nudge across, everything changes. Half-useless — you can localize it in one direction only.
- On a **corner**: nudge it in *any* direction and the content changes. Jackpot. A corner is pinned down in both $x$ and $y$.

The **Harris corner detector** turns this into math. Around each pixel, collect the image gradients $I_x, I_y$ over a small window $W$ and build the structure tensor

$$
M \;=\; \sum_{(u,v)\,\in\, W}
\begin{bmatrix}
I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\
I_x I_y & I_y^2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The two eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ of $M$ measure "how much the patch changes" along the two principal directions: both small → flat, one large → edge, **both large → corner**. Rather than computing eigenvalues explicitly, Harris uses the famous shortcut score

$$
R \;=\; \det(M) - k\,\mathrm{tr}(M)^2 \;=\; \lambda_1\lambda_2 - k(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^2,
\qquad k \approx 0.04,
$$

which is large and positive at corners, negative on edges, and near zero on flat stuff. OpenCV does the sliding-window part for us; we keep every pixel that beats its 8 neighbours, so a corner counts once, not fifty times:

```python
def harris_corners(gray):
    R = cv2.cornerHarris(gray, blockSize=2, ksize=3, k=0.04)
    R_dilated = cv2.dilate(R, None)          # 3x3 local max filter
    is_peak = (R == R_dilated) & (R > 1e-4 * R.max())
    ys, xs = np.nonzero(is_peak)
    scores = R[ys, xs]
    order = np.argsort(-scores)              # strongest first
    return np.column_stack([xs, ys])[order], scores[order]

pts1, sc1 = harris_corners(g1)   # ~14,000 candidate corners!
pts2, sc2 = harris_corners(g2)
```

## 3. Spread the love (ANMS) 🗺️

We have ~14,000 corners per image. We only need a few hundred, so let's keep the strongest… right?

Trap! Corner strength is clumpy. The ornate tower stonework generates hundreds of ultra-strong corners in one small area, so "top 300 by strength" gives you 300 points crammed onto the towers and *nothing* on the rest of the image:

<figure class="pano-figure">
  <img src={base + "images/pano/05-anms.jpg"} alt="Comparison: top 300 corners by strength cluster on the towers; 300 ANMS corners cover the whole image" />
  <figcaption>Left: strength-based selection throws a party on the towers. Right: ANMS makes everyone spread out.</figcaption>
</figure>

Why is clumping bad? Because we'll fit a warp for the *whole image* from these points. Fitting a global transform from points in one tiny region is like surveying one dorm and calling it a national poll.

**Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS)** fixes it with one elegant idea: for each corner $i$, ask

> *"How far away is the nearest corner that is meaningfully stronger than me?"*

$$
r_i \;=\; \min_{j}\;\lVert \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j \rVert
\quad\text{over } j \text{ such that } c\,R_j > R_i,
\qquad c = 0.9 .
$$

Call $r_i$ the corner's **suppression radius**. A corner with a big radius is the champion of a big neighbourhood — keep those. A strong corner sitting next to an even stronger one has a tiny radius — drop it. Then "keep $N$ corners" = "keep the $N$ biggest radii", and coverage happens automatically. (The $c = 0.9$ means "you only suppress me if you're at least 10% stronger" — it stops two nearly-tied neighbours from suppressing each other on a coin flip.)

```python
def anms(pts, scores, num_keep=500, c_robust=0.9):
    pts = pts[:1200].astype(np.float64)      # only consider the strongest 1200
    scores = scores[:1200]
    # d2[i, j] = squared distance from corner i to corner j
    d2 = ((pts[:, None, :] - pts[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
    # only distances to corners that are meaningfully stronger count
    meaningfully_stronger = scores[None, :] * c_robust > scores[:, None]
    d2 = np.where(meaningfully_stronger, d2, np.inf)
    radius = np.sqrt(d2.min(axis=1))         # suppression radius per corner
    radius[0] = np.inf                       # the strongest corner fears no one
    return pts[np.argsort(-radius)[:num_keep]]

keep1 = anms(pts1, sc1)   # 500 well-spread corners, left image
keep2 = anms(pts2, sc2)
```

## 4. Give every corner a name tag 🏷️

We have 500 corners in each image. Now, for each corner in the left image, we need to find *the same physical spot* among the right image's corners. To compare corners, each one needs a **descriptor** — a little numeric fingerprint of what the image looks like around it.

Dead-simple idea: grab the 40×40 pixel patch around the corner. Done?

Almost — raw patches are too *literal*. If the corner's position is off by two pixels (it will be) or the exposure differs (ours does!), raw patches disagree everywhere. So we rough them up on purpose:

1. **Blur** the patch (Gaussian, $\sigma \approx 2.5$) — small misalignments stop mattering.
2. **Shrink** 40×40 → 8×8 — keeps the gist, discards fragile detail. 64 numbers.
3. **Normalize** to zero mean and unit variance — and this is the magic one: for a patch $\mathbf{d}$, use $(\mathbf{d} - \mu)/\sigma$, and brightness shifts and contrast changes *vanish*. A dark version and a bright version of the same patch become the *same* descriptor.

```python
def make_descriptors(gray, pts, patch=40, out=8):
    blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(gray, (0, 0), 2.5)
    h, w = gray.shape
    descriptors, kept_pts = [], []
    for x, y in pts.astype(int):
        if not (patch//2 <= x < w - patch//2 and patch//2 <= y < h - patch//2):
            continue                          # too close to the border
        p = blurred[y - patch//2 : y + patch//2, x - patch//2 : x + patch//2]
        p = cv2.resize(p, (out, out), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA).ravel()
        p = (p - p.mean()) / (p.std() + 1e-8)   # exposure-proof!
        descriptors.append(p)
        kept_pts.append((x, y))
    return np.float32(descriptors), np.float64(kept_pts)

D1, kp1 = make_descriptors(g1, keep1)
D2, kp2 = make_descriptors(g2, keep2)
```

## 5. Speed dating for corners 💘

Time to match. For every left descriptor, compute its distance to *every* right descriptor (64-dimensional sum of squared differences), and note the **best** and **second-best** candidates.

Now the crucial bit. When is a match trustworthy? Not simply when the best distance is small — repetitive structures (windows, railings, ripples) produce *lots* of small distances. The real signal is when the best match is **much better than the runner-up**:

$$
\text{ratio} \;=\; \frac{d_{\text{best}}}{d_{\text{second-best}}} \;<\; 0.8 .
$$

> If your soulmate barely beats the second option, maybe don't propose. — David Lowe's ratio test, roughly

```python
d2 = ((D1**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (D2**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * D1 @ D2.T)
nearest2 = np.argsort(d2, axis=1)[:, :2]           # best & runner-up per row
best  = d2[np.arange(len(D1)), nearest2[:, 0]]
runup = d2[np.arange(len(D1)), nearest2[:, 1]]
ratio = np.sqrt(best / runup)                      # 0 = confident, 1 = coin flip

is_match = ratio < 0.8
match_p1 = kp1[is_match]                           # left points
match_p2 = kp2[nearest2[is_match, 0]]              # their right partners
```

## 6. Interlude: the warp we're hunting (homographies) 🌀

Before we can vote out the impostors, we need to know what the honest matches agree *on*. What's the actual relationship between the two photos?

When a camera **rotates in place** (your arm doing the pano wave), the two images are related by a **homography**: a 3×3 matrix $H$ that maps every pixel of one image to its position in the other, in homogeneous coordinates:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ w' \end{bmatrix}
\;\sim\;
\begin{bmatrix}
h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\
h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\
h_7 & h_8 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
(x', y') \leftarrow \left(\tfrac{x'}{w'},\, \tfrac{y'}{w'}\right).
$$

That divide-by-$w'$ is what makes a homography more powerful than rotate+shift: it can make parallel lines converge — the keystone "leaning building" effect — which is exactly what camera rotation does to the world.

$H$ has **8 unknowns** ($h_1 \dots h_8$). Each match pins down 2 equations (one for $x'$, one for $y'$), so **4 matches determine a homography**. Given 4+ matches, we build a linear system $A\mathbf{h} \approx \mathbf{0}$ and solve for the null space with SVD — the classic **Direct Linear Transform (DLT)**. The code is short (coordinate-normalization details in the <a href={base + "code/stitch.py"}>full script</a>):

```python
def apply_h(H, pts):
    """Apply H to Nx2 points, including the divide-by-w'."""
    p = np.column_stack([pts, np.ones(len(pts))]) @ H.T
    return p[:, :2] / p[:, 2:3]

def dlt_homography(src, dst):
    """Fit H such that dst ~ H @ src, from N >= 4 point pairs."""
    A = []
    for (x, y), (u, v) in zip(src, dst):
        A.append([-x, -y, -1,  0,  0,  0, u*x, u*y, u])
        A.append([ 0,  0,  0, -x, -y, -1, v*x, v*y, v])
    _, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(np.array(A))
    H = Vt[-1].reshape(3, 3)      # null space = the h that makes A @ h ~ 0
    return H / H[2, 2]
```

So: 4 good matches → the exact warp. But hand DLT even *one* impostor match and it will cheerfully produce a nonsense warp. With ~13% of our matches being impostors, least-squares over everything is doomed. We need something outlier-proof.

## 7. RANSAC, the democracy of matches 🎰

**RANSAC** (RAndom SAmple Consensus) is the algorithm equivalent of "let's just vote on it":

1. Pick **4 matches at random**. Fit a homography to just those 4.
2. Ask every match to vote: *warp your left point with $H$ — does it land within 4 pixels of your right point?* If yes, you're an **inlier** for this $H$.
3. Count votes. Remember the $H$ with the most.
4. Repeat a few hundred times. Finally, refit $H$ using *all* inliers of the winner — a big, stable least-squares, now safe because the liars are gone.

Why it works: impostor matches are *random* — they don't agree with each other. Honest matches all agree on the one true warp. So the moment your random sample happens to be 4 honest matches, hundreds of honest matches suddenly vote yes, and no coalition of impostors can ever match that score.

```python
best_inliers = None
for _ in range(600):
    sample = np.random.choice(len(match_p1), 4, replace=False)
    H = dlt_homography(match_p2[sample], match_p1[sample])
    projected = apply_h(H, match_p2)                  # warp right pts to left frame
    err = np.linalg.norm(projected - match_p1, axis=1)
    inliers = err < 4.0                               # pixels
    if best_inliers is None or inliers.sum() > best_inliers.sum():
        best_inliers = inliers

H = dlt_homography(match_p2[best_inliers], match_p1[best_inliers])  # final refit
```

How many spins do we need? If a fraction $w$ of matches are honest, one sample of 4 is all-honest with probability $w^4$, so after $k$ spins

$$
P(\text{never saw a clean sample}) \;=\; \left(1 - w^4\right)^k .
$$

With our $w \approx 0.87$: one spin is clean with probability $0.87^4 \approx 57\%$, and the failure probability after just 10 spins is about $0.02\%$. That's the deep trick of RANSAC — you don't need most samples to be good, you need **one**, and one comes fast. (Real systems solve this formula backwards to pick the number of iterations.)

RANSAC is bigger than panoramas — it rescues *any* model-fitting from outliers. Here it is saving a humble line fit; run it, then crank `N_OUTLIERS` and watch least-squares fold while RANSAC shrugs:

## 8. Warp it 🌀

We have $H$. Time to actually *use* it: warp the right image into the left image's coordinate frame and lay them on a shared canvas.

One subtlety worth knowing — warping runs **backwards**. Instead of pushing each right-image pixel to its new home (which leaves holes), we go through every pixel of the *output*, apply $H^{-1}$ to ask "where in the right image does this come from?", and sample there. `cv2.warpPerspective` does this for us. We just have to compute a canvas big enough to hold both images: warp the 4 corners of the right image, take the bounding box, and shift everything by a translation $T$ so nothing lands at negative coordinates.

```python
corners = np.float64([[0, 0], [W, 0], [W, H_img], [0, H_img]])
warped_corners = apply_h(H, corners)
x0, y0 = np.floor(np.minimum(warped_corners.min(0), 0)).astype(int)
x1, y1 = np.ceil(np.maximum(warped_corners.max(0), [W, H_img])).astype(int)
T = np.array([[1, 0, -x0], [0, 1, -y0], [0, 0, 1.0]])   # keep pixels positive

canvas_size = (x1 - x0, y1 - y0)
warped2 = cv2.warpPerspective(im2, T @ H, canvas_size)
canvas1 = np.zeros_like(warped2); canvas1[-y0:-y0+H_img, -x0:-x0+W] = im1
```

## 9. Blend away the evidence 🪄

The last impostor is the seam itself. The classic fix is **feathering**: in the overlap, cross-fade between the images — but do it smartly. Each pixel gets a weight equal to its **distance to the edge of its own image** (a distance transform gives us this in one call). Near your own border you whisper; deep inside your own territory you shout:

```python
def feather_weight(mask):
    return cv2.distanceTransform(mask.astype(np.uint8), cv2.DIST_L2, 5)

w1 = feather_weight(mask1)                # mask1/mask2 = where each image has pixels
w2 = feather_weight(mask2)
total = np.maximum(w1 + w2, 1e-6)
panorama = (canvas1 * (w1/total)[..., None] +
            warped2 * (w2/total)[..., None]).astype(np.uint8)
```

## 10. The victory lap 🏁

The whole pipeline, one breath: **Harris** found thousands of recognizable points; **ANMS** kept 500 well-spread ones; blurred, shrunken, normalized **descriptors** made them comparable across exposure changes; the **ratio test** matched them; **RANSAC** voted out the 19 impostors; **DLT** fit the homography from the 129 honest witnesses; **warpPerspective** bent the right photo into place; and **feathering** erased the seam.

A fun sanity check: our two views were derived from one wide photo with a known warp, so we can grade ourselves — the recovered homography lands the image corners within **1–3 pixels** of the true positions. From nothing but pixels — not bad.

### Challenges, if you're feeling dangerous 😈

- **Your own photos.** Take two shots out your window (rotate, don't sidestep — a homography assumes rotation about the camera). Feed them to the <a href={base + "code/stitch.py"}>script</a>.
- **Three or more images.** Stitch neighbours pairwise and chain the homographies onto one canvas. What slowly goes wrong as the chain grows?
- **Gain compensation.** We hid the exposure difference with feathering; instead, estimate a brightness scale between images from the overlap and correct it before blending.
- **Level up the blend.** Feathering can ghost when alignment is imperfect — implement two-band (Laplacian pyramid) blending and compare.
- **Level up the features.** Swap our 8×8 patches for `cv2.SIFT_create()` and re-run the pipeline. What survives rotation and zoom now?
